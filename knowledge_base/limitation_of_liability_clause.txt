Limitation of Liability Clause Reference

Limitation of liability clauses cap or exclude the monetary damages a party
can recover for breach of contract or other specified claims. These are among
the most heavily negotiated provisions in commercial agreements because they
directly allocate risk between the parties.

Types of limitations: Liability caps set a maximum monetary amount the
liable party must pay. Common caps include a multiple of fees paid, a fixed
amount, or a formula based on contract value. Exclusions of liability remove
entire categories of damages, typically consequential, indirect, incidental,
special, or punitive damages. Some clauses also exclude loss of profits, loss
of business, loss of data, or loss of reputation.

Unenforceable exclusions: Certain liability exclusions are unenforceable in
most jurisdictions. These include liability for death or personal injury caused
by negligence, fraud or fraudulent misrepresentation, gross negligence or willful
misconduct, and breach of confidentiality or data protection obligations.
Statutory consumer protection rights cannot be excluded.

Standard carve-outs: Carve-outs preserve liability for specific categories even
when general limitations apply. Common carve-outs include indemnification
obligations, intellectual property infringement, breach of data protection
obligations, breach of confidentiality, unauthorized use or disclosure, and
payment obligations. The scope of carve-outs determines how much risk the
liability cap actually covers.

Exclusive remedy provisions: Limitation clauses often state that the specified
remedies are the exclusive remedies available to the non-breaching party. This
prevents the non-breaching party from seeking additional remedies outside the
contract. Exclusive remedy clauses should be carefully evaluated in conjunction
with liability caps.

Mutuality: Limitation of liability clauses may be mutual or one-sided. Mutual
limitations apply equally to both parties. One-sided limitations apply only to
one party, typically the vendor or service provider. Courts may scrutinize
one-sided limitations, especially where the parties have unequal bargaining
power.

Indemnification interaction: Liability caps may or may not apply to
indemnification obligations. The contract should clearly state whether
indemnification payments are subject to the general liability cap. If
indemnification is carved out, the indemnifying party carries uncapped
liability for indemnified claims.

In contract review, examine the cap amount, the exclusions and carve-outs, and
whether the limitations are mutual. Consider whether the cap is proportionate
to the potential loss and whether the exclusions of consequential damages are
appropriate given the nature of the contract. Verify that mandatory exceptions
for death, personal injury, fraud, and gross negligence are preserved.
