Termination Clause Reference

Termination clauses define the circumstances and procedures under which a
contract may be ended before the completion of its full term. These clauses are
critical for managing risk and providing exit mechanisms when contractual
relationships break down.

Termination for cause: Also known as termination for breach, this allows a
party to terminate if the other party commits a material breach. Most clauses
require the Breaching Party to receive a cure period, typically 15 to 60 days
after written notice, to remedy the breach. If the breach continues beyond the
cure period, the non-breaching party may terminate. The clause should define
what constitutes a material breach, including failure to pay, failure to
perform core obligations, breach of confidentiality, and breach of data
protection obligations. Incurable breaches may justify immediate termination
without a cure period.

Termination for convenience: This allows a party to terminate the contract
without cause by providing advance notice, typically 30 to 90 days. Termination
for convenience provides flexibility but can disadvantage a party that has made
investments in the relationship. Some contracts limit termination for
convenience to specific phases or require payment of termination costs.

Termination for insolvency: Most contracts permit termination if the other
party becomes insolvent, files for bankruptcy, makes an assignment for the
benefit of creditors, or has a receiver appointed. These provisions protect
against the risk of non-performance due to financial distress.

Notice requirements: Termination must be effected by written notice specifying
the grounds for termination. The notice period, delivery method, and any cure
rights must comply with the contractual requirements. Electronic delivery is
often acceptable if the contract does not specify a particular method.

Post-termination obligations: After termination, the parties must fulfill
outstanding obligations. Common post-termination duties include payment of fees
accrued before termination, return or destruction of confidential information,
cooperation on transition, and completion of in-progress work where
practicable. Survival clauses specify which provisions continue after
termination, typically confidentiality, limitation of liability,
indemnification, and dispute resolution.

Effects of termination: Termination discharges future performance obligations
but does not affect rights and obligations that accrued before termination.
The contract should clarify whether termination is without prejudice to
remedies for prior breach.

In contract review, assess whether cure periods are commercially reasonable,
whether termination for convenience is mutual or one-sided, whether
post-termination obligations are clearly defined, and whether the survival
clause covers all necessary provisions.
