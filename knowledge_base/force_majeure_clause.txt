Force Majeure Clause Reference

A force majeure clause excuses a party from performing its contractual
obligations when an extraordinary event beyond its control prevents
performance. These clauses are common in commercial contracts and have received
increased attention following global disruptions.

Definition and scope: Force majeure typically covers events such as natural
disasters, war, terrorism, pandemics, government actions, strikes, and
supplier failures. Some clauses specifically list covered events while others
use a general definition. The broader the definition, the more likely a party
can successfully invoke the clause. Narrowly tailored lists limit protection to
only those events explicitly named.

Legal standards: Most jurisdictions require that the event be unforeseeable at
the time of contracting, unavoidable despite reasonable precautions, and
external to the affected party. The burden of proof rests on the party invoking
the clause. Common law systems tend to interpret force majeure clauses strictly
while civil law systems may have statutory force majeure concepts that apply
even without an express clause.

Notice requirements: Force majeure clauses usually require the affected party
to provide prompt written notice to the other party. Notice deadlines vary from
immediately to within a specified number of days. Failure to provide timely
notice may waive the right to claim force majeure.

Effects on obligations: During a force majeure event, the affected party is
typically excused from performance for the duration of the event. Some clauses
suspend all obligations while others only suspend the affected obligations. The
non-affected party may be entitled to suspend its own counter-performance.

Mitigation obligations: Many clauses require the affected party to take
reasonable steps to mitigate the impact of the force majeure event and resume
performance as soon as possible. Failure to mitigate may limit the scope of
the excuse.

Duration and termination: If the force majeure event continues beyond a
specified period, either party may usually terminate the contract. Termination
rights may be mutual or unilateral. Post-termination obligations such as return
of confidential information and payment for work completed should still apply.

Commercial considerations: Parties should negotiate the list of force majeure
events carefully. Supply chain disruptions and pandemic-related events should
be explicitly addressed. Exclusion of certain events such as supplier failure
or economic hardship may shift risk significantly. Insurance coverage should be
considered alongside force majeure provisions.
